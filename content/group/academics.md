Fountainhead runs the full IB continuum. A child can move from their first years of school through to a university-entrance qualification without changing the way they are taught — the same conviction about how children learn, carried across three programmes.

## The continuum

- **Primary Years Programme** — transdisciplinary inquiry, ages 3–12.
- **Middle Years Programme** — concept-driven and interdisciplinary, ages 11–16.
- **Diploma Programme** — six subject groups plus the core, ages 16–19.

Each programme has its own page with the detail that matters to a parent deciding: age range, how learning is structured, and how it is assessed.

## IB and parents' role in education

The IB framework places parents at the centre of a child's learning — not as passive observers, but as active partners. At Fountainhead, we believe the school and parents are strongest when they work together.

**What the IB asks of parents**

The IB is not a curriculum you can outsource to a school and a tutor. It asks children to inquire, to reflect, to communicate, and to act. These habits do not form in school alone — they form in the conversations at dinner, in the books that are available at home, in the questions a parent asks rather than the answers they provide.

**How parents can support IB learning at home**

- Attend Parent Orientation sessions — the IB is taught differently from traditional curricula, and understanding the approach helps parents support rather than undermine it.
- Support self-study and homework by asking the right questions, not by doing the work for the child.
- Build a reading culture at home — read aloud, make books available, find reading groups.
- Expose children to a wide variety of experiences beyond the standard routine — travel, museums, community work, conversations with people from different walks of life.
- Model the values you want your child to develop: integrity, respect, commitment, and tolerance.
- Avoid over-scheduling — children need unstructured time for play, reflection, and rest. This is not a gap in their development; it is part of it.
- Trust the process. The IB develops skills that are not always visible in short-term grades but are consistently evident in how students perform in university and beyond.

**What parents should avoid**

The IB is undermined — not helped — when parents arrange tuition with teachers whose approach contradicts the school's pedagogy, when children are coached to reproduce answers rather than think through problems, or when parents resolve difficulties for the child rather than letting the child work through them with support.

The school's role is to teach. The parent's role is to nurture. The child's role is to learn. When all three are played well, the results are consistently strong — and more importantly, lasting.
